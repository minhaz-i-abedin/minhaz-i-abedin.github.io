Edu_game

Send current from the package bump down through the metal stack to the transistor. Every wire and via costs resistance. Every neighbor costs capacitance.

Made by Minhaz and Claude. Free to reuse for educational purposes.

How to play

Back-end-of-line (BEOL) is the stack of copper wiring built above a chip’s transistors. Thin, tightly packed wires lose voltage to resistance and talk to their neighbors through capacitance. Each level asks you to trade one against the other. See the research →

  • Move: arrow keys or WASD, or tap the next junction next to the marker.
  • Inspect: tap any wire to read its resistance.
  • Trade-off labs: use the sliders in the highlighted Your controls box above the drawing. The parts you control are outlined with a dashed line, and every design you try is plotted below.
  • Score: up to 3 stars per level. Using a hint caps a level at 2 stars.
Physics notes and limits
  • Series and parallel: within one path the vias and wires are in series, so their order does not matter and the total is just the sum. The three bumps feed the same transistor through three separate paths that are in parallel, so the current divides by 1/R and the transistor sees less than the best single path. Each path ends in its own contact plug on a wide shared drain.
  • S/D contact: the transistor contact here is a source/drain plug on the diffusion, where DC current flows. A MOS gate draws no DC current, so IR drop would not apply to it. A gate net is an RC delay problem instead (see the Lab levels).
  • Vias in parallel: n vias give less than an n-fold drop because the current crowds into the nearest one. The game uses neff = n / (1 + 0.1 (n − 1)).
  • Narrow copper: resistivity rises from surface and grain-boundary scattering, and the liner takes cross-section. The Route and Widen tables use fixed room-temperature values, which are also lower than the same wires would have at 105 °C (copper is about 30 % higher).
  • Electromigration: Black's equation gives the median life, so the labs design to the 0.1 % early-failure tail. Not modeled: the Blech effect (short wires are immune), Joule self-heating, and the series weak-link statistics of a net with many segments.
  • Coupling capacitance: a worst-case Miller factor of 2 is applied to a neighbor that switches against the victim; a quiet or grounded neighbor counts once and a neighbor switching the same way would count zero. The score is the effective load, which sets delay, not the crosstalk noise ratio Cc/(Cc + Cg).
  • Low-k penalty: the resistance penalty for porous and air-gap films is a teaching heuristic for process damage, not a measured value.
